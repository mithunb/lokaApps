Two doors, one ruler

Three different machines group the same 66 Bengaluru places today: the key switches count the columns, the theme-finder invents a new column, and the label shelves arrange 335 written words. This page argues they need one shared ruler, not one shared machine — and shows the ruler working on the real data.

The rule a person can hold: a key is a short list a stranger could learn, that says something about nearly every pin. Everything else people wrote stays a word — you tap it, or search it. A key's words still work as taps: becoming a key is a promotion, never a move.

1 · The ruler, run over every real column

Every column is measured the same two ways — how many kinds, and how many places the commonest eight kinds describe. The measurement decides the door. Click a row for the reasoning.

These are the actual numbers from the Bengaluru layer, not examples.

categories 10 kinds · the top eight describe 63 of 66 places KEY — and its words stay tappable

A stranger can learn “Culture, Nature, Heritage, Market, Learning, Activities, Civic, Food and Drink” in one read, and the list says something about nearly every pin. That is a key. Tapping “Culture” on a place still narrows the map — the promotion takes nothing away.

creator 5 kinds · they describe all 66 places KEY

Five names, every place covered. One name covers 59 of the 66 — so the map will look mostly one-coloured, and that is the truth of the data. The ruler lets an honest lopsided column through; the stricter gate that refuses lopsided sets polices invented sets only. Facts are allowed to be lopsided; inventions are not.

themes (when kept) 2–7 kinds by construction · leftovers capped, no kind may cover more than about half KEY — born fitting the ruler

The theme-finder is the ruler run forwards: it manufactures a column to the same measurements the key test checks for — few kinds, most places described, honest leftovers. A kept theme column should therefore always qualify as a key. If it ever doesn't, that is a bug in the plumbing, not a judgment call.

labels 335 kinds, 303 written exactly once · even the eight commonest touch only 19 of 66 WORDS — tap & search, shelved to browse

Nobody learns 335 words, and a colouring built from the top eight leaves most of the map grey. But every one of the 335 is a precise question the map can answer — “who shares aerial-prop-roots?” So the labels keep their own door: tappable everywhere, searchable, and arranged on shelves for browsing. This is not a demotion; it is the door where 335 words are strong.

address 54 kinds, 47 written once SEARCH ONLY

Too many kinds for a key, and no one browses a list of addresses — but typing “Palace Rd” should find the place, and it does. Some columns are only ever answers to a typed question.

created_at 47 different time-stamps · not words a person would read or type NEITHER — bookkeeping

Refused by both doors, correctly: time-stamps aren't readable kinds and would let a stray digit match everything in search. (Someday dates could power a third door neither key nor search — “show me this map through time” — but that door doesn't exist yet and shouldn't be faked with this one.)

What “make this a key” means: one new column in your table, kept or discarded by you — exactly like a found theme. Membership is exact: a place sits on a shelf only because of words it already carries, so every claim can be checked by tapping the shelf's own labels. The 231 quieter labels stay browseable below. A place can sit on two shelves — 20 of these do — and the marks beside a pin already know how to wear more than one answer.

And when the shelves don't add up — when they describe less than most of the map — no offer appears. Shelves stay a way to browse. The ruler decides, the owner keeps or discards, and nothing is ever forced into a colouring.

What this page is arguing. Keep three makers — people's own columns, the theme-finder, the label shelves — because they make three different promises. Give them one ruler (the counting above) and one sentence on the panel, so a reader always knows whether a word colours the map or narrows it. The only new machinery is the bridge in section 3: when shelves happen to describe most of the map, the ruler notices and makes an offer. The owner's say-so stays where it is — nothing becomes a column without a person keeping it.
