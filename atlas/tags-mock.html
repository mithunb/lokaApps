What the labels can do — two ideas, shown with the real layer

Every place, label, family and count on this page comes from the live Bengaluru layer: 66 places, 335 distinct labels, 374 label mentions. Nothing is invented. The matches in the first idea were found by shared words for this page; the atlas's own matching goes by meaning, so the real thing would only be better.

Idea 1 — “More like this” on every place's card

Pick any of the 66 places. Its card grows one small row: up to three places that feel most like it, and a word on what links them. Tap one to travel there. When nothing is truly close, the row stays away — no forced matches (where “close enough” sits is a dial; on this page it is set for the rougher word-matching used here). This needs nothing new: the atlas already keeps a meaning-summary of every place for search; this row just asks which summaries sit closest, once, when a layer is saved.

Why this beats what exists: today the only way from one place to the next is a category (Culture holds 32 of 66 places) or a theme (Urban Green Spaces holds 27). This row is place-to-place: the recycled sofa leads to the paint-brush chair; the banyan leads to the other banyan.

Try the honest edge: pick the egg-rice stall — one of a kind, so its row hides itself.

Idea 2 — “What people notice here”: the layer's self-portrait

All 374 noticings, sorted into families of meaning, biggest first. This is the page the map cannot show pin by pin: what this city's walkers find worth writing down. Tap a family to open it; tap a label to see which places carry it — on the real map, that tap would simply run the search that already works. Sorting the labels into families happens once per layer: automatically once labels arrive carrying their meaning (a short note the computer keeps about what a phrase means, so it can tell carved-stone and granite shrine speak of the same thing), or until then by the same kind of pass — and the same keep-or-discard say — that themes already get.

ONE-OF-A-KIND NOTICINGS — 303 of the 335 labels appear at exactly one place; these are the reason label-equals-label matching was rightly refused
Why the pins keep their colours: a place's labels usually span two or three families (only 27 of 66 places have even three-quarters of their labels in one), and the leading family often describes the decoration, not the place — the recycled library's labels would paint it “Mood & charm”. Grouping places is themes' job, and themes already do it well here (62 of 66 housed). These two ideas put the labels to work at the level where they are strong: label to label, and place to place.
