Rows of shapes beside the pin, chips that light the map

Mock only. Nothing here is built, and no live file changed. The owner's design, in its two halves. Keys wear rows: the pin stays the plain 20 px circle, and beside it sits one small row per switched-on key — each key in its own shape. The first key is a row of circles, the second a row of squares (then triangles, diamonds, bars, the crown's own shapes). Within a row, one mark for every answer the place holds: two categories are two circles, side by side. Groups are chips: they never mark the pin. They live with search, and tapping one lights the places it fits — the rest stay on the map, dimmed.

On this layer that means two rows at most: categories (circles — half the places hold two, some three) and themes (one square). Every size below is true to the screen; every mark is the floor-measured size the corner marks already passed (a circle 12, a square 10.6 — each carrying at least a 12 px dot's worth of nameable colour). All numbers were measured on the real 66 Bengaluru places with the fold rule every earlier verdict used.

1 · One place, one key at a time — a row per key

A real place — the recycled library in Yeshwanthpur: two categories (Social, Culture) and one theme (Recycled Design). Top row true size, bottom three times enlarged. With nothing on, the pin claims nothing; each key switched on adds one row, in that key's shape.

2 · The typical place and the widest place — and the crown beside both

With both keys on, the typical pin here carries three marks (measured: 2.9 on average; 37 of 66 places carry exactly three) and the widest carries four — three categories and a theme, true of 12 places. The crown beside each shows what it shows today: one corner mark per key, the first answer only. Everything at true size, then enlarged.

The place that was this design's monster has shrunk: with groups moved into the chips, the National Gallery of Modern Art — ten marks in the everything-gets-a-row version — is now four (Culture, Nature, Social; Creative Hubs). Its six groups wait in the chips row below the search box, where they light it up instead of hanging off it.

3 · Which row is which key — the shape says so

A row of marks could belong to any key; the shape answers it, exactly as it does on the crown. The key panel pairs each key with its shape in words and pictures, rows sit in the panel's order, and resting on any pin repeats every switched-on key in words. The panel miniatures are the same marks the panel draws today, above the colour floor (measured before, unchanged) — the shrink-to-a-sliver failure that killed the ring-of-parts does not return. Shapes follow the order keys are switched on, the crown's own rule: the first key on wears circles, the second squares — and the panel always says which is which.

4 · If a row is ever too wide — show every mark anyway

On this data the question retires: the widest real row is three circles — 40 px, and no place exceeds it. But the rule should exist before the data that needs it. Both escapes were measured on the heavier three-row version of this design, where rows reached six marks: capping at four (three shown + “+2”) and wrapping to a second line each changed not a single fold count, at any zoom, at either density — the places that fold are folded by the typical block, not the rare wide one. And shrinking marks below their floors makes colour nobody can name. So the rule is: a row shows every mark it has. Hiding answers behind a “+2” is the crown's failure crept back in, and it buys nothing measurable.

5 · Left or right of the pin — right, flipping at the map's edge

Rows sit to the right of the pin — the side the eye reads on to. A pin near the map's right edge flips its block to the left; nothing else changes, and the marker's width — the thing folding cares about — is the same either side. Drawn with a real place: the handmade leather-puppet stall, the layer's easternmost point.

6 · Groups are chips — search apparatus, and a tap lights the map

The fourteen groups live beside the search box, each with its true count. Tapping one emphasises: places in the group keep their full ink, every other place stays on the map but fades. Nothing is removed, nothing re-folds, no pin jumps — which also means a small group (Play & sport holds 5 places) lights its five without emptying the map, where hide-the-rest would leave 61 places gone. Tap the chip again to put it down; one chip at a time, and typing a search would release it. Try it below — the map is the dense neighbourhood with both keys on.

← the shipped search box; the chips sit with it

Can the shipped machinery dim instead of hide? Yes, cheaply. Search already decides place-by-place who matches (word-boundary match on the layer's own words) and then hides the rest; the deciding half is reused as it stands. Only the last step changes: instead of taking non-matching pins off the map, a chip press paints them faint — one new marker state beside “hidden”, one line where markers are painted, one style rule. Folded discs need one rule of their own: a disc stays lit while any place inside it is lit, and fades only when none is. If dimming were refused and chips had to hide instead, the costs would be real: the map re-folds on every tap (pins jump), a small chip empties the map, and “5 of 66 shown” reads as loss where “5 lit” reads as light.

8 · The honest ledger

What this design spends and what it buys, measured on the real 66 places. The three-row version of this design — before groups moved into the chips — is in the last row as a reminder of what was avoided.

For colour-blind readers

Which key a row belongs to is a shape, not a colour — circles against squares, fully readable without colour, the same channel the crown offered. Within a row, marks differ only by colour, but the eight key colours were chosen to stay tellable apart under the two common kinds of colour-blindness (measured before: every pair stays clearly separate) — so a reader can see that a place holds two different categories even when naming them takes the hover or the panel, same as today. And the failure the three-row version carried — two or three identical grey marks in one row, on 15 places — is gone: with only categories and themes wearing rows, no place wears two greys in one row, and only 9 places wear any grey at all. Use the COLOUR SIGHT switch above to judge the map through those eyes.

What the crown hides that the rows show

The crown wears one mark per key — its first answer. On this layer 49 of 66 places hold two or three categories, so with both keys on the crown mis-tells three places in four: Culture-and-Nature wears only Culture. The rows show every answer, and the cost measured honestly is close to nothing at two keys: one place better in the deciding scene, three places worse across the whole layer at z16, one worse at z17. The place a reader taps is told the same either way — the card always shows everything — but the map itself stops needing the correction.

Said plainly: measured on his own yardstick — readable places at real density — the two-row design draws level with the crown, and it no longer lies. Grown neighbourhood at z16: plain 19, crown 13, rows 14 (16 by the kinder rectangle measure). Whole layer today: crown 33 / rows 30 at z16, 43 / 42 at z17, 24 / 24 at z15. In exchange the marker stops hiding the second and third categories that 49 of 66 places genuinely hold, keeps a shape channel per key for colour-blind readers, redraws truthfully at panel size, and never shows the unreadable grey pairs the three-row version wore. The one state where the crown is still plainly tighter is a single key on: its lone 34 px mark keeps 16 places in the deciding scene against the rows' 14, and 34 / 49 across the layer against 30 / 42 — a row of two circles is simply wider than one corner mark, and that is the price of showing both categories rather than the first. The earlier three-row version of this design lost outright (7 kept against the crown's 13); moving groups into the chips is what rescued it — and the chips give the groups a better home than a row ever was: fourteen names with counts, one tap, and the places light up without the map losing anyone else.
