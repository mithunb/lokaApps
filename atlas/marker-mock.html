The pin as a plain pin — and whether its body can carry the keys

The decision being explored: a pin means only “a place is here.” The little picture inside it goes, no key starts switched on, and everything a place is — its kind, its theme, who added it — appears only when the reader turns a key on.

The question this mock answers with pictures and measurements: once the inside of the pin is free, can the pin’s own body carry the keys — a ring cut into parts, a body split into slices, rings inside rings — instead of the small corner marks that ship today? Every size below is true to the screen, and every verdict comes from the same measuring sticks the corner marks already passed: each key’s colour patch must carry at least the ink of a 12 px dot, in a blob at least as chunky as the flattest shipped mark. Marks that fail show a colour the eye cannot name — the map would lie.

Measured, and worth pausing on: with the picture gone, the thin coloured rim is all that is left — and after the screen smooths its edges, a 2 px rim keeps not a single pixel of nameable colour (its chunkiest solid patch measures 0 px against a floor of 6). If a key is on, the freed inside must carry the colour: either a solid dot in the middle (the same 12 px of ink every corner mark carries) or the whole body filled. Otherwise the map shows a colour nobody can read.

3 · The merging problem: colours repeat, and neighbours touch

The shipped rule says colours may repeat between keys — the shape of a mark says which key it belongs to. Inside one body, the parts have no shape of their own, only a position; when two neighbouring parts land on the same colour they fuse into one part unless a wide white joint holds them apart. This is not rare: on the real Bengaluru layer, 53 of 66 places would have at least one pair of same-colour neighbouring ring parts with three keys on, and with two keys as a split body, 13 of 66 places would show both halves in one colour — a marker that looks exactly like a one-key pin.

4 · The key panel has to teach which part is which — and it can’t, at panel size

Whatever the map draws, the key panel and the hover bubble must repeat in miniature. The crown’s little shapes survive shrinking: a small square drawn at panel size still carries an 8 px blob of colour — above the floor. A ring part drawn at panel size thins to a 4.5 px sliver — below the floor the map itself obeys. And the words get worse: “small squares” names a shape; a ring part can only be named by clock position.

The ring’s honest win, said plainly: at three keys it is ~26 px wide against the crown’s ~40, so across the whole layer at this zoom it keeps 40 places readable where the crown keeps 33 (51 against 43 one zoom in). That is the whole case for the ring — and sections 3 and 4 are what it costs. At five keys even this win disappears.

6 · What the icon was doing, and what remains when it goes

The picture inside the pin said the kind directly — a leaf meant nature before any colour was read. That mattered most to readers who cannot tell some colours apart. With the icon gone and no key on, the pin claims nothing, so nothing is lost. With a key on, the colours doing the work are the eight chosen precisely because they stay apart under the two common kinds of colour-blindness (measured: every pair stays clearly separate). And the words remain: resting on any pin names the place and answers every key that is on, in words. Use the COLOUR SIGHT switch above to check the map through those eyes.

What is honestly lost

Kind-at-a-glance, without touching the map. A leaf said “nature” from across the room; a green dot says it only to readers who can read green, and says it to everyone else after one hover. That is the price of the pin becoming just a pin — worth stating once, plainly, and it is the owner’s call that the cleaner map is worth it.

The recommendation, in one breath: keep the round 20 px pin (phone-taken spots are only sure to about a pin-width anyway — at this zoom a pointed tip would claim a pixel the data doesn’t know); let the plain pin be white with the layer’s one colour; when the reader turns the first key on, let the freed inside carry that key — a solid colour dot where the icon used to be. That far, the owner’s container instinct is right and measured. Beyond one key, every way of carving the body — ring parts, slices, rings inside rings — either breaks the colour floors the shipped marks were built on, or merges on more than half the real layer, or cannot be repeated at key-panel size. The corner marks stay the honest way to carry keys two to five: they keep full-size colour patches, their shape names their key, and the panel can draw them truthfully. The ring’s only real win — a thinner marker at exactly three keys — buys seven more readable places out of 66 and spends the pin’s own identity to get them.
