Colour For pins, colour goes entirely — the panel's key switches do that job for every reader now. Colour stays only where colour is the message: the ramp on a shaded map, and the one ink a line or fill has.

1 · The colour controls, judged one kind at a time

“The colour control” is really four questions asked of four kinds of layer. A pin's colour is a costume; a shaded map's colour is its content. So the verdict differs by kind — judged on what each control actually paints, measured on the live map.

PinsBengaluru Finds — both your layers
“Colour places by …” goes the five flat colours goes
The key switches took this job. Any reader can colour the map by any column that counts well — categories, creator, themes — and stack up to five. The owner's pick only decided which key came first. And the flat colour ever painted just a thin ring on a white pin and one dot in the key: from town height every group folds into a moss disc, so the choice is invisible where people actually look.
Circles sized by a numbera table of amounts
“Size circles by …” stays circle colour goes
Which number sets the size is what the map says — that is not a colour control and it stays. The circles' colour is the same costume as a pin's ring; the upload flow never even asked for it.
Shaded areasa number shading each block
“Shade areas by …” stays the colour ramp stays
On a shaded map, colour is the content. Blues that deepen with rainfall read right; sand-to-brown says the opposite of the same numbers. Picking the ramp is picking the meaning, so it is the one colour choice that must survive.
Lines & fillsa drawn river, a boundary file
the one colour they have stays
Their only dial, and it means something — water wants slate, never rust. Take it away and fixing a wrong-coloured river means uploading the file again. Honest note: no atlas has an added line or fill layer yet, so keeping this costs you nothing — it never shows on a pin layer.

4 · What it costs

In attention

Readers
Nothing new. The panel they see is unchanged — switches, keys, the browse door.
You
The Change card drops from seven things to four on a pin layer. Theme-finding is one quiet line per layer you may change, silent until pressed. Each atlas shows it at most a few times, and it retires itself once themes are kept.

In AI calls

Opening or unfolding the door
Free. Everything shown before the run comes from data the page already has.
Read the places (66 places)
3 calls, about half a minute — one to find the themes, two to file each place into them. An honest “no clear themes” costs the same. Nothing is saved between runs, so each press pays again.
Keep these themes
1 more call today — the way back in re-reads the table as if it were new. Probably avoidable; flagged for the code, not for you.
Browse all labels (the shelves)
1 call ever per layer. The arrangement is kept beside the atlas; everyone after the first visitor reads it for free.
  • Homes it refused: under Change (where it hid — your note is the proof) · a sheet of its own listing everything the data could say (a third place answering “what can colour this map”, which the switch list already answers — the exact mistake this product keeps having to unmake) · the strip (the strip answers for the whole atlas; themes belong to one layer).
  • The shelves stay a separate door. Browsing labels changes nothing and is for everyone; finding themes changes the layer and is yours. Same shelf, two doors, each named for its question.

Still yours to decide

  • Lines, fills and ramps: I recommend their colour controls stay (they are those layers' whole voice, and they never appear on your pin layers). Say the word and they go too — the cost lands on whoever uploads a river file and wants it un-rust.
  • The door's words: “Find themes with AI…” said here; your phrase “discover underlying themes” works too — same door either way.
  • Finding again: once themes are kept the door retires. If new places arrive later and you want a “find again”, that is a separate decision — not built here.
